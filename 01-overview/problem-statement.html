Problem Statement

Context

Sound, as we commonly experience it, consists of pressure waves oscillating at frequencies the human ear can detect — roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Below this audible range lies infrasound: atmospheric pressure waves that oscillate at extremely low frequencies, approximately 0.01 Hz to 20 Hz.

Simple Analogy: A normal microphone is like a fast camera — it captures rapid vibrations in air. An infrasound sensor is like a time-lapse camera — it captures extremely slow atmospheric pressure changes that happen over seconds or even minutes.

These very-low-frequency pressure waves are generated by powerful natural and human-made events:

  • Volcanic eruptions
  • Large meteorological disturbances (severe storms, hurricanes)
  • Meteor entries into the atmosphere
  • Large explosions
  • Rocket launches
  • Ocean wave interactions (microbaroms)

Infrasound waves can travel hundreds or even thousands of kilometres through the atmosphere because low-frequency waves experience less atmospheric absorption than audible sound. This makes infrasound monitoring valuable for detecting distant events that are impossible to hear.

The Problem

1. Detection Is Technically Difficult

At 0.01 Hz, a single pressure oscillation takes 100 seconds to complete. The pressure variations involved can be extremely small — often fractions of a Pascal against a background atmospheric pressure of approximately 101,325 Pa. Detecting a signal that is many orders of magnitude smaller than the background, and that changes over timescales of seconds to minutes, is fundamentally harder than detecting audible sound.

2. Normal Microphones Are Insufficient

Standard microphones are designed for the audible frequency range (20 Hz – 20 kHz). They typically:

  • Have a high-pass frequency response that rolls off below ~20 Hz
  • Are not designed for the sensitivity and stability needed at sub-hertz frequencies
  • Lack the DC-coupled or quasi-DC signal path required for very slow pressure changes
  • Do not include mechanisms to suppress slow barometric drift

3. Wind Noise Dominates

Wind flowing over a sensor port creates turbulent pressure fluctuations. These fluctuations can be orders of magnitude larger than the infrasound signal of interest, especially at frequencies below a few hertz. Without dedicated wind-noise reduction, the signal is buried in environmental noise.

4. Temperature and Barometric Drift

Over minutes and hours, atmospheric pressure changes due to weather patterns, diurnal heating, and altitude variations. Temperature changes also affect sensor electronics and mechanical components. These slow drifts must be suppressed or compensated, otherwise they overwhelm the infrasound measurement.

5. Existing Systems Are Complex and Expensive

Professional infrasound monitoring systems — such as those operated by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization (CTBTO) International Monitoring System (IMS) — use research-grade microbarometers, large spatial-filtering pipe arrays (sometimes spanning 1–3 km), and sophisticated data processing infrastructure. These are designed for treaty verification and scientific research, making them inaccessible for educational and small-scale applications.

Proposed Problem Statement

There is a need for a practical, low-cost prototype system capable of:

  1. Detecting atmospheric pressure variations in the 0.01–20 Hz frequency range
  2. Reducing wind and environmental noise using a spatial-averaging manifold
  3. Suppressing slow barometric drift through differential-pressure design with a reference chamber
  4. Digitizing the signal with sufficient resolution and sampling rate
  5. Processing the digital signal using filtering, FFT, and spectral analysis
  6. Extracting meaningful features from the processed signal
  7. Detecting anomalous patterns using AI-based anomaly detection
  8. Visualizing results on a real-time dashboard with alerting

The system should be accessible enough for student teams and hackathon projects while being technically sound enough for educational research and atmospheric monitoring exploration.

What This Problem Statement Does NOT Claim

  • It does NOT claim the system will replace professional-grade infrasound networks
  • It does NOT guarantee detection of specific event types (explosions, meteors, etc.)
  • It does NOT claim the AI will classify events — the MVP focuses on anomaly detection
  • It does NOT assume the prototype will achieve professional noise-floor specifications

Success Criteria

The prototype successfully addresses this problem statement if it can demonstrate:

  1. Measurable response to controlled low-frequency pressure variations
  2. Visible reduction in noise when wind-noise reduction is engaged
  3. Clean digitized waveform display
  4. Correct FFT/spectral analysis output
  5. AI anomaly detection flagging controlled test signals as anomalous
  6. Dashboard displaying real-time data and alerts
